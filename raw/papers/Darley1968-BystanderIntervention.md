# Bystander Intervention in Emergencies: Diffusion of Responsibility

**Authors**: John M. Darley (New York University), Bibb Latané (Columbia University)  
**Journal**: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968, Vol. 8, No. 4, pp. 377-383.  
**DOI**: 10.1037/h0025589  

---

## Abstract
Ss overheard an epileptic seizure. They believed either that they alone heard the emergency, or that 1 or 4 unseen others were also present. As predicted the presence of other bystanders reduced the individual's feelings of personal responsibility and lowered his speed of reporting (p < .01). In groups of size 3, males reported no faster than females, and females reported no slower when the 1 other bystander was a male rather than a female. In general, personality and background measures were not predictive of helping. Bystander inaction in real-life emergencies is often explained by "apathy," "alienation," and "anomie." This experiment suggests that the explanation may lie more in the bystander's response to other observers than in his indifference to the victim.

---

## Key Methodology and Experimental Data
- **Background**: Kitty Genovese murder in NYC (1964) with 38 reported non-intervening witnesses. Public discourse blamed "apathy", "moral decay", or "alienation". Authors propose a situational mechanism: *Diffusion of Responsibility*.
- **Sample**: 59 female and 13 male introductory psychology students at New York University (NYU). Two subjects excluded for seeing through the deception, leaving N = 70.
- **Experimental Paradigm**: Intercom discussion of college problems in individual cubicles. The perceived group size was manipulated via tape recordings:
  - 2-person group (Subject + Victim)
  - 3-person group (Subject + Victim + 1 Confederate)
  - 6-person group (Subject + Victim + 4 Confederates)
- **The Emergency**: Victim speaks first, hesitantly reveals susceptibility to seizures under stress. On round 2, victim undergoes an apparent severe epileptic seizure (stuttering, crying out "I'm gonna die... help...", choking sounds, silence after 125 seconds).
- **Core Results (Table 1)**:
  - 2-person: 85% reported before cut-off (125s), 100% ever responded; mean response time = 52s; speed score = 0.87.
  - 3-person: 62% reported before cut-off, 85% ever responded; mean response time = 93s; speed score = 0.72.
  - 6-person: 31% reported before cut-off, 62% ever responded; mean response time = 166s; speed score = 0.51.
  - Differences in response likelihood: $\chi^2 = 7.91, p < .02$.
  - Differences in speed score: $F = 8.09, p < .01$.
- **Individual Differences & Group Composition**:
  - Bystander sex (male vs female) and medical competence (premedical student) had no significant effect.
  - Subject sex had no effect (males 69%, females 62%).
  - Personality scales (Machiavellianism, anomie, authoritarianism, social desirability, social responsibility) showed no predictive correlation with helping speed.
- **Psychological State of Non-responders**:
  - They were NOT apathetic or uncaring; they exhibited severe emotional conflict (avoidance-avoidance conflict), sweating palms, trembling hands, and anxious inquiries after the experiment.
