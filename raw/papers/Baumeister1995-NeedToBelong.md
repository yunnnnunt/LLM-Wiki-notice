# The Need to Belong: Desire for Interpersonal Attachments as a Fundamental Human Motivation

**Authors**: Roy F. Baumeister (Case Western Reserve University), Mark R. Leary (Wake Forest University)  
**Journal**: Psychological Bulletin, 1995, Vol. 117, No. 3, pp. 497-529.  
**DOI**: 10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.497  

---

## Abstract
A hypothesized need to form and maintain strong, stable interpersonal relationships is evaluated in light of the empirical literature. The need is for frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing relational bond. Consistent with the belongingness hypothesis, people form social attachments readily under most conditions and resist the dissolution of existing bonds. Belongingness appears to have multiple and strong effects on emotional patterns and on cognitive processes. Lack of attachments is linked to a variety of ill effects on health, adjustment, and well-being. Other evidence, such as that concerning satiation, substitution, and behavioral consequences, is likewise consistent with the hypothesized motivation. Several seeming counterexamples turned out not to disconfirm the hypothesis. Existing evidence supports the hypothesis that the need to belong is a powerful, fundamental, and extremely pervasive motivation.

## Core Theoretical Framework & Empirical Synthesis

### 1. The Belongingness Hypothesis: Two Essential Features
To satisfy the fundamental need to belong, an individual requires:
1. **Frequent, affectively pleasant or positive interactions** with a few other people.
2. These interactions must occur in the context of a **temporally stable, enduring framework of mutual affective concern and caring**.
*(Partial satisfaction: Interactions without a bond of caring, like prostitution or casual business dealings, or an ongoing bond without contact, like prison or involuntary long-distance separation, leave the need unsatisfied).*

### 2. Nine Metatheoretical Criteria for a Fundamental Human Motivation
a) Operates across a wide variety of situations;  
b) Produces strong affective consequences (joy/relief when formed, anxiety/depression/grief when broken);  
c) Directs cognitive processing (attributions favor in-group/partners, cognitive merging of self and other);  
d) Leads to severe ill effects (pathology, mental illness, immune system degradation, elevated mortality) when thwarted;  
e) Elicits goal-oriented behavior subject to satiation (diminishing returns beyond a few close bonds) and substitution;  
f) Is universal across all cultures;  
g) Is non-derivative (not a byproduct of hunger, sex, or power);  
h) Influences a broad variety of human behaviors;  
i) Has implications extending beyond immediate psychological functioning.

### 3. Key Empirical Findings Synthesized
- **Forming Social Bonds**: Occurs spontaneously and easily (Tajfel's minimal group paradigm, Robbers Cave study). Threat and shared adversity strongly accelerate bond formation.
- **Reluctance to Break Bonds**: Universal distress at divorce, bereavement, or group dissolution, even when relationships are objectively destructive or toxic (e.g. battered women).
- **Cognition**: Self-serving biases extend to close others; transactive memory systems in close relationships; spontaneous social categorization.
- **Emotion**: Social exclusion is the primary trigger of social anxiety and depression.
- **Deprivation Consequences**:
  - Lynch (1979): Mortality rates across all causes significantly higher among unmarried, unattached individuals.
  - Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1984, 1987): Immunocompetence (natural killer cell activity) depressed in lonely or maritally disrupted individuals.
  - Durkheim (1897/1963): Suicide rates heavily inversely linked to social integration.
- **Cross-connections to Social Psychology Classics**:
  - Explains conformity: Normative compliance in Asch paradigms is driven by the terror of social rejection.
  - Explains group aggression: Gangs, lynch mobs, and military atrocities (Browning, 1992) stem from group loyalty and fear of exclusion rather than innate cruelty.
  - Resolves the bystander dilemma: Cohesive groups do NOT exhibit bystander apathy (Rutkowski et al., 1983); the bystander effect is an artifact of disconnected strangers.
