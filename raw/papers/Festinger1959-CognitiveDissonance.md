# Cognitive Consequences of Forced Compliance

**Authors**: Leon Festinger, James M. Carlsmith (Stanford University)  
**Journal**: Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1959, Vol. 58, No. 2, pp. 203-210.  
**DOI**: 10.1037/h0041593  

---

## Abstract
What happens to a person's private opinion if he is forced to do or say something contrary to that opinion? Only recently has there been any experimental work related to this question. Two studies reported by Janis and King (1954; 1956) clearly showed that, at least under some conditions, the private opinion changes so as to bring it into closer correspondence with the overt behavior the person was forced to perform. Kelman (1953) found that a large reward produced less subsequent opinion change than did a smaller reward. Festinger (1957) proposed a theory concerning cognitive dissonance from which come a number of derivations about opinion change following forced compliance. In this experiment, 71 male students performed repetitive, boring tasks for an hour and were then paid either $1 or $20 to tell a waiting girl that the tasks were enjoyable and lots of fun. Consistent with cognitive dissonance theory, participants paid $1 rated the tasks as significantly more enjoyable (+1.35) than those paid $20 (-0.05) or controls (-0.45). The greater the reward offered beyond what was necessary to elicit behavior, the smaller was the subsequent opinion change.

## Full Methodological & Empirical Details

### 1. Theoretical Derivation
- Let private opinion be $X$, overt statement be $\text{not } X$.
- Dissonance $D$ vs. Consonance $C$. Total magnitude of dissonance:
  $$\text{Magnitude of Dissonance} = f\left(\frac{D}{D + C}\right)$$
- If overt behavior is induced by rewards or punishment threats, dissonance is maximal if reward was just barely sufficient to induce the statement. As rewards become larger, consonance increases and dissonance becomes smaller.
- Pressure to reduce dissonance leads to private opinion change. Hence, the larger the reward, the smaller the opinion change.

### 2. Sample and Exclusions
- 71 male introductory psychology students at Stanford University.
- 11 subjects excluded from analysis:
  1. 5 subjects (3 in $1, 2 in $20) suspicious of being paid to tell the girl the experiment was fun.
  2. 2 subjects (both in $1) told the girl they were hired to lie and it was really boring.
  3. 3 subjects (1 in $1, 2 in $20) refused to take money and refused to be hired.
  4. 1 subject ($1) asked the girl for her phone number to explain things later.
- Final analyzed sample: $N = 60$ (20 in Control, 20 in $1 condition, 20 in $20 condition).

### 3. Experimental Procedure
- **Tasks (60 min total)**:
  - Task 1 (30 min): 12 spools on a tray, empty tray, refill tray, using one hand at own speed.
  - Task 2 (30 min): Board with 48 square pegs, turn each peg a quarter turn clockwise, using one hand at own speed.
- **Manipulation**:
  - Control: Sent directly to interviewer.
  - $1 Condition: Paid $1 (and signed handwritten receipt) to tell waiting female confederate that tasks were interesting, enjoyable, and exciting.
  - $20 Condition: Paid $20 to do the same.
- **Confederate Interaction**: Confederate girl mentions her friend said the experiment was boring. Subject responds and attempts to convince her. Audio recorded via hidden tape recorder.
- **Post-experimental Interview (Four Questions on 11-point scales)**:
  - Q1: How enjoyable tasks were (-5 to +5):
    - Control: -0.45
    - One Dollar: +1.35 ($t = 2.48, p = .02$ vs Control; $t = 2.22, p = .03$ vs $20)
    - Twenty Dollars: -0.05 (Not significant vs Control)
  - Q2: How much they learned (0 to 10): Control 3.08, $1: 2.80, $20: 3.15 (Non-significant, $t = 0.48$).
  - Q3: Scientific importance (0 to 10): Control 5.60, $1: 6.45, $20: 5.18 ($t = 1.79, p = .08$).
  - Q4: Desire to participate in similar experiment (-5 to +5): Control -0.62, $1: +1.20, $20: -0.25 ($t = 1.78, p = .08$).

### 4. Rebuttal of Janis & King Mental Rehearsal Alternative Explanation
- Hidden recordings analyzed by two independent raters on 5 dimensions (content before remark, content after remark, overall content, persuasiveness/conviction, time spent).
- In all cases, $20 subjects were rated slightly higher than $1 subjects (e.g. persuasiveness 5.50 vs 4.79). Thus, $1 subjects did NOT improvise more or argue more convincingly; the dissonance reduction mechanism is uniquely supported.
